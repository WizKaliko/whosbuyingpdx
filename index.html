Partners Against Progress

They aren't Republicans. Some of their donors are.

These races carry no party label. But 125 individual donors to six of Portland's business-backed candidates have also written checks to Republicans like Christine Drazan. Among the individual donors of the four progressive candidates we checked, we found none.

We know these candidates are Democrats. That's the problem.

Republicans can't win a council seat in Portland, so they don't need to. Conservative Democrats can tell the Republican story about this city, with some Republican donors behind them, and lend it local credibility.
Why it matters

The real primary is on your city ballot.

The state

Oregon is effectively a one-party state. Its Democratic primaries are rarely real contests: insiders usually beat outsiders.

The fight

So the fight over the party's direction plays out in nonpartisan city and county races, with no party label on the ballot.

The money

One side is Portland's conservative candidates: business-backed Democrats whose politics have made them a magnet for Republican donors, people who have written checks to Christine Drazan, Knute Buehler and other Portland conservatives.

The contrast

The four progressive candidates' individual donors who did the same: zero.

Definition

Whose votes these are

Clark and Zimmerman side with the conservative bloc on about 95% of split votes.

Novick is the wild card. He breaks with them on police masking and ID rules and some other policing votes, but sides with them too often when the money is on the table. Conservative donors are finding him.

Brim-Edwards voted for $101.6M in county money for the same arena.

Arnold has no votes yet. He's a sitting Portland police officer who says the city "has fewer sworn officers than peer cities" and makes police staffing a priority. In 2021 he used force in 18 incidents, the most of the 465 officers who reported force that year; in 16, the bureau listed the person as transient. He tied for fifth in 2023. Ctrl-Alt-Defund analysis of Police Bureau data. His donors have the highest Republican and conservative rate of any candidate we checked: 44%.

Torres has no record in office yet. She's backed by the Chamber's committees.

Who's buying

Much of their outside support runs through one pipe: the United for Portland Action Fund, the Chamber's PAC. Realtors' and developers' money goes in. It comes out as the Coalition for Portland's Future and as billboard committees with neighborhood-sounding names.

A complaint filed Oct 5 says the billboard setup hid where the money came from. That's an allegation; no one has ruled on it.

The wild card

Steve Novick's votes haven't moved right. His new donors lean that way.

On the council record, Novick hasn't drifted right. But the people who gave to him for the first time in 2025–26 were more than three times as likely to have backed Republicans or Portland conservatives as the people who first gave in his 2023–24 campaign. We can't say why: 2025–26 is also when the Chamber's committees and Go Portland Go endorsed him.

5 · The economics

An old theory with a Portland address.

The Chamber's agenda follows the same theory as trickle-down economics: keep taxes low at the top and prosperity spreads to everyone. That idea has been tested for 50 years.

Who gets the gains

Top 1% share of pre-tax income including capital gains (Piketty–Saez series, from 1913). 2021 set the record (23.6% in 2022), above the peak on the eve of the Great Depression. Source: Emmanuel Saez, UC Berkeley, 2024 update. The top 1% also holds 32.5% of U.S. wealth, the most since Fed tracking began in 1989 (Federal Reserve). In Oregon, the top 1% took about 14% of income in 2023, nearly as much as the bottom half (Oregon Center for Public Policy, from state revenue data, source).

What the research found

50 years, 18 countries. Major tax cuts for the rich raised the top 1%'s share of income, with no significant effect on growth or unemployment. London School of Economics

Since 1945 in the U.S. Lower top tax rates tracked with income concentration, not growth. Congressional Research Service

Kansas, 2012. Deep cuts didn't beat neighboring states' growth; schools and roads were cut, and a Republican legislature reversed most of the cuts in 2017. Brookings

The 2017 federal tax cut. Gains went mostly to owners and top earners. Wages rose about $750 per worker, against $4,000 or more promised. Investment rose (about 11% at exposed firms), but typical workers saw little. AEA · Harvard Gazette

The Chamber says

"Portland has lost approximately $1 billion in taxable income annually since 2020."

2026 regional policy agenda
What the data shows

High-earning households grew. Census survey data show Multnomah households earning $500,000+ rose from about 2,500 in 2019 to about 7,700 in 2024 (small samples). Portland State University

Green's office: the leavers' income jump tracked pandemic market gains. An analysis by Councilor Mitch Green's senior policy analyst, released ahead of a county vote, tied it to investment gains seen nationwide, including in Seattle, which has no state income tax. Green's office

Millionaires rarely move for taxes. Tax Policy Center

The honest caveat. An independent 2026 working paper did find some extra departures after Preschool for All, costing about 8.5% of its revenue (the authors' estimate; not yet peer reviewed). Some people left; the revenue effect was modest. Reed College

To be fair: the Chamber backs some public spending, including the parks levy (Chamber) and, as a top 2026 priority, public money for the Moda Center renovation (Chamber). It doesn't use the words "trickle-down"; that's our description of an agenda built on cutting and capping taxes at the top. Inequality has many causes, and tax policy is one that research ties to it.

6 · The doom loop

Portland isn't failing. It's recovering unevenly.

The Portland Metro Chamber gave the city its scariest phrase, and the groups funding the committees behind these candidates keep telling the same story of decline. Even Mayor Wilson pushed back, urging "boom loop thinking" WWeek.

Partnership for Progress's newsletters have featured what the Mercury calls "satirical, AI-generated images that spin narratives of Portland in decline" (Mercury).

Those are real problems to solve. They aren't proof that Portland is failing, and they aren't an argument for handing the city to the people selling the doom.

Where it comes from

Oregon's ORESTAR campaign-finance filings, Portland's council voting records, and news coverage linked at each claim.

What counts

A person counts if they gave to the candidate and, since 2006, to one of 18 Republican campaigns or party committees, and the street address, or ZIP and employer, match. The same test runs on every candidate shown.

What we don't claim

The candidates are Democrats. Republicans aren't funding or endorsing them as a party, and many prominent Democrats back them. When we call them conservative, we mean where they stand in Portland politics, judged by their votes, their backers and their message, not their party registration.
