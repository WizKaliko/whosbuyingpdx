Kellie Torres

challenger

The Chamber's PAC and a police-union-backed PAC back her. She was the only D3 debater to say yes to moving PCEF climate money into police hiring.

Who they are

Torres manages external affairs and partnerships for Portland Parks & Recreation. She was chief of staff to Commissioner Dan Ryan, and before that an elementary school teacher and an educational-equity leader at All Hands Raised; this is her first run for office (Mercury, Sept 9, 2026). WWeek says she was Ryan's chief of staff from 2020 to 2024 and that her Parks job was created to seek public-private partnerships for a $700M maintenance backlog (Oct 14, 2026). She has worked for the city on and off since 2005 (OPB, Oct 7, 2026) and calls herself one of the "practical, progressive leaders who deliver results" (campaign site).

The problem

Kellie Torres has never held office, so her case rests on three things: who is spending to elect her, what she has said she would do, and what she has chosen not to say. Start with the backers. United for Portland, the Portland Metro Chamber's political arm, tells District 3 voters to rank her first, Novick second, and "not rank any other candidates" [1]. Unity PAC, a committee of the Portland Police Association, the firefighters, building trades, the labor council and United for Portland, recommends only Novick and Torres and reported $210,000 owed or spent with a Washington, D.C., firm [2]. Go Portland Go, funded by Partnership for Progress and Columbia Sportswear CEO Tim Boyle, endorsed her too [3]. Her donors include Chamber President Andrew Hoan and her former boss, Councilor Dan Ryan [4]. Her recorded answers match those backers' positions on police funding. At the Oct 7 City Club debate, asked whether she would vote for an initiative moving 25% of the voter-approved Portland Clean Energy Fund into hiring about 400 police, she said "Yes"; every other District 3 candidate on stage said no [5]. On the Fair Share tax, a proposal to raise about $72 million a year from roughly 400 large corporations while cutting taxes for 18,000 small businesses [6], she said at the debate she was "not sure" and wanted to know more [5]; at the North Tabor Neighborhood Association's Oct 13 forum, her answer was recorded as No [7]. She was recorded as supporting the Moda Center term sheet [7], which commits $120 million up front and up to $275 million for maintenance of the Blazers' arena [8]. She was recorded as opposing Measure 26-267 [7], which would let residents decide 2% of discretionary spending [9]. Where progressives want specifics, her platform is quiet. Portland Civic Lab found no answer from her on new revenue, camp-removal funding or Moda public money in her pamphlet, site or questionnaires [10]. Her police plan is to fill funded positions, then "consider additional officers"; the measures it tracks are vacancies, hiring and response times, not oversight [11]. Her answer to tight budgets leans on "grants, philanthropy, sponsorships" [11], which Civic Lab notes do not provide stable operating revenue [10]. She was chief of staff to Commissioner Dan Ryan from 2020 to 2024 [12]. In that period Ryan cast the deciding vote against Jo Ann Hardesty's $18 million police cut [13], co-wrote the 2022 plan to ban street camping and build camps of up to 250 people [14], voted for the 2023 daytime camping ban with fines or jail [15], and opposed the charter reform voters passed [16]. Those are his votes, not hers, and no source shows her role in them. Independent reviewers have weighed in. The Mercury called her "the establishment pick to help turn Council back to the status quo" [17]. Willamette Week, which ranked her third, said her "policy ideas" were not "terribly original" [12]. There is a fair defense: she takes public matching funds and no direct PAC money [10], and she supports a 24/7 Portland Street Response and rent assistance [7][11]. But the Chamber is asking voters to rank her instead of both District 3 progressives, and on climate money for police, the arena and resident budgeting she took the other side from both [1][7][5].

Police plan: fill funded jobs, then consider more officers

Her platform would fill the police positions already funded, then consider more officers "based on workload, response times and investigative needs." It sets no police-accountability or oversight goals; the metrics it tracks are vacancies, hiring and response times.

Opposes Measure 26-267, residents deciding 2% of budget

Her recorded answer was No on the participatory budgeting charter amendment, under which residents would make binding decisions on at least 2% of discretionary General Fund spending (about $16.4M in FY 2027-28). Mayor Wilson, her endorser, is part of organized opposition to the measure.

What they'd say

Torres is in the Small Donor Elections program; Civic Lab's reading of her filings shows $0 in direct PAC or labor-committee money and a $500 largest business gift, and independent committees can back her without her consent. She has 21 years in public service and support from former Gov. Barbara Roberts, firefighters and the building trades. At the North Tabor forum she said Yes to expanding Portland Street Response and CHAT to 24/7 and to using PCEF to restore TriMet service, and her platform backs rent assistance, eviction prevention and legal aid (Civic Lab; North Tabor NA; campaign site). On the Fair Share tax she said at the debate she "could support it" for its small-business relief and wanted to know more, and she cites helping build Safe Rest Villages as shelter work. Ryan's votes as commissioner were his, not hers. Novick, whom many progressives rank, also answered Yes on the Moda term sheet.
